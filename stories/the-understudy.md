Marguerite had known the part of Lady Bracknell for eleven years before she ever set foot on stage as her. She knew it the way you know a hallway in the dark — not because she'd memorized it, but because she'd walked it so many times waiting in the wings that her feet simply stopped asking her brain for directions.

She was forty-three, and understudies, as a rule, are not forty-three. They are twenty-six and hungry and willing to sit through a hundred rehearsals for the ninety seconds a season when the lead loses her voice. Marguerite had done the math once, on a train, out of boredom: fourteen productions, four understudied roles, one hundred and twelve performances watched from a folding chair backstage, and exactly three nights on.

Tonight would be the fourth.

"She's not going to make it," said Tom, the stage manager, not looking up from his clipboard. "Fever's real. I heard her through the dressing room door."

"I heard the pipes through the dressing room door once and thought it was a ghost," Marguerite said. "Doesn't make it true."

But it was true. Twenty minutes to curtain, and the woman who had originated the role — twenty-nine, luminous, effortlessly cruel in the way the part demanded — was lying on a cot in wardrobe with a washcloth over her eyes, apologizing to no one in particular.

Marguerite did not feel the thing she'd always assumed she'd feel. Not triumph, not vindication, not even nerves, exactly. She felt the particular calm of a person handed a familiar tool. She put on the dress. It fit differently than it had in fittings — costumes always did, once you knew you were actually going to wear them out there, in front of people who'd paid to sit in the dark and be told a story.

In the wings, waiting for her entrance, she caught her own reflection in the black glass of a monitor and didn't recognize herself for exactly one second, which she had learned, over eleven years, was the correct amount.

"A handbag?" she said, when her cue came, and the line landed the way it was supposed to land — surprised laughter, a little ripple through the house that she felt on the back of her neck like a change in weather. She had said the words in her own kitchen a thousand times, to no one, while doing dishes. She had never once said them to two hundred strangers who had no idea she'd been waiting this long, or that waiting had, in its own unglamorous way, been its own kind of preparation.

Nobody in that audience knew her name. By the time she took her bow — third from the end, the understudy's traditional place — most of them still didn't, and wouldn't, and that was fine. She wasn't performing to be known tonight. She was performing because the part had been quietly, patiently hers all along, and it had simply taken the room eleven years to notice.

Backstage after, Tom handed her a paper cup of water like it was a medal.

"Same time next week?" he asked, not entirely joking.

"Same time whenever," she said, and meant it as the truest thing she'd said all night.
